Section 01 · The idea

Hybridized Wisdom

TURAL is a family of perforated shell pods standing on screw piles in 2 to 6 m of water on a degraded reef flat. The base of each pillar is a printed ceramic reef collar, so the building's foundation is the ecological payload rather than the ecological cost.

In plain words

Imagine a small restaurant standing on legs in shallow sea water. The underwater part of each leg is made of fired clay with a special texture that baby corals like to grow on. The floor has holes so sunlight still reaches the corals. All pipes and cables leave sideways through the walkway, so nothing drips into the sea. And after 10 pm the lights go out, because baby turtles and spawning corals both find their way by darkness.

The name comes from ทะลุ (talu, "pierced through"), the rock arch that names Koh Talu. The pods are pierced too: perforated deck, oculus, open grooves. Piercing lets light, water, larvae and air pass through a building that sits on a living surface.

The three lines the project runs on

Line 1

The foundation is the reef

Take the dome and the deck away and what remains is more reef than was there before. The collar is roughly 1 to 3 % of pod cost Estimate, which is the argument for why every shore structure should carry one.

Line 2

Talu means pierced through

ทะลุ is the island's own word. The pods are pierced too: perforated deck, oculus, open collar grooves. Piercing lets light, water, larvae and air pass through.

Line 3

Negotiated, not imposed

Every dimension in the book is set by something non-human: a tide level, a light threshold, a larval groove width, a turtle's eye. Each design decision is visible as an answer to one of those.

"Designs must result from negotiation rather than domination among human wisdom, ecological processes, climate systems, AI and computational models."ASA Experimental Design Competition 2026 brief, "Hybridized Wisdoms" Sourced

The Thai profession's stated 2026 agenda is almost a written brief for this project. Writing the thesis in that register means it lands with a Thai jury without translation.

Section 02 · The site

Koh Talu, เกาะทะลุ

A small private island 8 km off Bang Saphan Noi with a shallow fringing reef that recruits new coral, a 25-nest hawksbill season, a reef flat that dries once a year, and a decade of local restoration work already in the water.

In plain words

Koh Talu is a small resort island 20 minutes by speedboat from the mainland. Its reef was badly damaged by hot water (bleaching) in 2018 and 2024, but a June 2025 survey found it is recovering by itself: new baby corals are settling. Rare hawksbill turtles nest on its southern beaches, and a local foundation has been replanting coral there for over ten years. One strange fact drives the whole design: once a year in July, the lowest tide exposes part of the reef to air for a few mornings. Anything meant to grow coral must sit below that level.

The single most design-critical fact

Parts of the reef flat go subaerial (above water) once a year. Any pillar base carrying reef substrate must sit below that level.

Bleaching timeline

The hawksbill programme (เต่ากระ, Eretmochelys imbricata)

The island foundation established a rare sea turtle programme in 2011 with the Sea Turtle Conservation Center and Royal Thai Navy units. It is a true head-start programme: hatchlings are reared and released at about 25 cm carapace length, microchipped and registered in a global database. Nesting is on the southern beaches, roughly May to October; late-season nests hatch into the November to February window.

In the 2568 BE (2025) season, nest number 25 hatched as the last of the season. One nest hatched with only 16 % survival, which prompted more intensive nursery care. About 25 hawksbill nests a season is nationally significant: hawksbill nesting in the Gulf is rare. Sourced

Design consequence

No artificial light spill toward the southern shore, a seasonal restriction on night operation, and the strongest single move available: site the pods away from that shore entirely. A seasonal-closure calendar on a board reads as professionalism, not as a limitation.

Why Koh Talu is the right pilot, in one line

The reef is already degraded, destructive fishing has been stopped, a turtle hatchery operates, and restoration is already underway. TURAL is not built on a pristine reef. It is built on the rubble field, which is exactly where the guidance says to build.

Section 03 · Process

What changed since v0

Three decisions were reversed after research, and showing the reversal is worth more to a jury than hiding it, because visible process is one of the things Thai schools reward. This page is a deliberate part of the portfolio, not an apology.

In plain words

The first version of the boards (v0) had three ideas that the research could not support: coral growing on printed plastic, blue night lights to "feed" the coral, and a big number of corals planted that no one could confirm. Each was replaced with something that can be defended, and the change itself is shown on the board so the jury can see that research moved the design.

Change 01 · Material

PVC print → fired ceramic

Why. PVC has two completely different uses on a reef. As a temporary nursery scaffold it is standard practice, including at Koh Talu. As a permanent, load-bearing, colonisable surface it is indefensible: it leaches phthalates over decades, a grooved printed surface is near a worst case for particle release, and the biofilm on plastic is not the coralline-algae community larvae cue on.

The one-liner. PVC is a good scaffold and a bad reef.

Kept. A small PVC-frame nursery beside the service pod, labelled temporary. That turns a critique into a two-tier system diagram.

Change 02 · Light

Blue LED → dark sky, amber

Why, in four parts. (1) Wrong variable: aquarium blue is a daytime spectrum with a strict dark night. (2) Wrong magnitude: an architectural LED is far too weak at 3 to 6 m to add photosynthesis, but sits exactly in the 0.01 to 1 lux range corals read as a moon cue. (3) Wrong biology: wild corals are asked to spawn; aquarium corals are not. (4) Wrong site: blue is the worst spectrum for turtle hatchlings and travels furthest in seawater.

The elegant part. Amber is absorbed within 1 to 2 m of the surface, so the turtle-safe specification is automatically the coral-safe specification.

Change 03 · Numbers

"40,000 corals since 1997" → citable figures

Why. The figure could not be verified from any source. A number without a citation is the one thing a research-led board cannot survive.

Use instead. 3,600 fragments planted by DMCR in Sept 2017; "over a decade" of foundation coral work; about 25 hawksbill nests in 2025; 92.3 % Gulf bleaching in 2024; survey depth 1 to 5 m at 29 °C, June 2025.

Also retire. Any island area figure and any resort award claim.

Kept from v0, unchanged

  • The turtle-shell dome. It survives because it does work: compression geometry, rain catchment over 430 m² of plan area, deep shade over the deck, and a Buddhist reading (the tortoise drawing in its senses) that describes a real decision about an inward, quiet room over a loud sea.
  • Species-keyed grooves. The idea was right; only the numbers needed sourcing. They now come from two published studies and are keyed to the corals that actually recruit at this site.
  • The glass walkway and glass floor panels. Kept, with the maintenance consequence drawn rather than assumed, and with no light in them after dark.
  • Community jobs. Made specific: the foundation, Bang Saphan fishers, the resort's dive operation, and an established Thai ceramic community for the tiles.
  • The café above the reef. Promoted: it became a cluster of dining pods plus a service pod, which is what makes the module repeatable.

How to present this on a board

Three columns, each with the v0 drawing at small scale on the left, an arrow, and the v1 drawing on the right, with one sentence of reason underneath. Do not redraw v0 more beautifully than v1. The point of the page is that the research moved the design.

  • One small comparative section: PVC frame on the left, ceramic collar on the right, same scale, same depth, same coral.

Section 04 · Context

Thailand direction: the blue-tech coast

Every live Thai instrument in 2025 and 2026 points the same way, from net zero 2050 to a sovereign blue bond to marine spatial planning, and TURAL's Thai-ness is carried by material, fabrication and water logic rather than by applied ornament.

In plain words

Thailand's government, tourism authority and universities are all pushing in one direction: cleaner energy, a healthy sea economy, and coral repair after the 2024 bleaching. TURAL fits that direction. It is "Thai" not because it has Thai decoration, but because it is built the way Thai fishing jetties and stilt houses have always been built: on piles, with a walkway first, taking apart and living with the water instead of fighting it.

Anchoring under a fixed platform is precisely what a piled structure offers that a loose module cannot. Cite the failure, then answer it.

  • Chao Lay, 2025 paper. A modular system of intersecting horizontal and vertical elements designed to be renewed, replaced, assembled and disassembled; 52 to 80 % of structures orient to the sea.   • เรือนไทย (ruean thai). The stilt house: raised floor, deep eave, prefabricated panel-and-peg assembly, seasonal water tolerance. The pegged demountable panel is the historic Thai precedent for prefabrication.

  • Boonserm Premthada. Dorfman Prize 2019; Venice Biennale 2025 Special Mention for the Elephant Chapel, a 4 m arch of bricks made from elephant dung. Proof that a Thai architect wins internationally by turning a local waste stream into structure. Sourced

The kitsch line

Banned: a dome literally shaped and coloured as a carapace with head and flippers; applied ลายไทย decals; gold trim; a chofa on a tech building; a naga at the entry; teak-and-orchid resort Thai-ness. The test: if you removed the symbol, would the building still work the same way? If yes, it is decoration.

Certification, honestly

TREES-NC, the Thai Green Building Institute rating, has eight credit categories. TURAL meets or exceeds TREES-NC intent in six: building management, water conservation, energy and atmosphere, materials and resources, indoor environmental quality, green innovation. The site and landscape category has no marine equivalent: its credits are written for stormwater, heat island, parking and transit, and several prerequisites assume a plot, a road and a municipal connection.

The board line

Section 05 · Structure

The module: pillar, reef collar, deck, shell

A diver-installed helical screw pile carries a ceramic reef collar through the splash and growth zones to a perforated deck and a shell, chosen so the whole assembly can be unscrewed and taken away again while the collar stays.

In plain words

Each pod stands on legs that are screwed into the sand like giant corkscrews, by divers, with no hammering. The part of the leg near the surface is where salt water eats steel fastest, so that part is wrapped in a clay collar. The collar does two jobs at once: it protects the steel and it is the textured surface where coral grows. Six water levels decide every height in the building, from where the floor must be (above the biggest storm wave) to where the collar must be (below the lowest tide, so it never dries out).

Section 06 · Ecology

Reef collar science

A coral planula is a one-millimetre larva that has to find a surface, read a chemical cue, metamorphose and then survive a year, and each of those four steps answers to a different property of the substrate, so the collar is specified in millimetres rather than adjectives.

In plain words

A baby coral (a planula) is about the size of a grain of sand. It floats, then has to land somewhere, decide the spot is good, glue itself down and change shape, and then not get eaten or buried for its first year. Two published experiments tell us exactly which surface shapes help: tiny ridges (2.5 mm high, 7.5 mm apart) make larvae settle about ten times more often, and small grooves (4 mm wide, at least 6 mm deep) triple their chance of surviving the first year. Fired clay is chosen because it is chemically neutral, unlike fresh concrete, which is briefly as alkaline as drain cleaner.

The numbers, and where they come from

  • Settlement. Levenstein et al., PLOS ONE 2022: ridges 2.5 mm tall at 7.5 mm spacing produced about ten times the settlement of flat substrate, extending the settling window by ≈ 300 %. Validated at 3 to 5 cm s⁻¹ oscillatory flow, realistic for this site. Sourced
  • Post-settlement survival. Randall et al., Frontiers in Marine Science 2021, 376 days: grooves 4 mm wide, 6 mm deep, cut through, gave 39 % mean spat survival against 13 % on flat controls. Sourced
  • Orientation. Settlement is strongly suppressed on sediment-laden surfaces. Horizontal, upward-facing grooves silt up. Vertical and downward-facing overhung grooves stay clean and are the classic natural recruitment microhabitat. This is a formal design move on the pillar, not a detail. Sourced
  • Cross-taxa caveat. Those studies used Caribbean brain corals and Acropora tenuis; Koh Talu is Porites, Pavona and Favites. The cues are hydrodynamic and refuge effects rather than species-specific chemistry, so they carry across. Section 07 · Light

Daylight budget by day, dark sky by night

Two light problems sit on top of each other: by day the deck must not starve the collar of the daylight coral and coralline algae need, and by night the building must not put any light at all into a sea where hatchlings and spawning corals both navigate by darkness.

In plain words

By day the building is a shade problem: coral and the pink algae that tell baby corals where to land both need sunlight, so the floor must let at least 70 % of the light through and the pods must be spaced so their shadows sweep across the sea instead of sitting still. By night the building is a light problem: even a faint glow confuses turtle hatchlings (they walk toward the brightest horizon) and shifts the night that corals release their eggs. So the only lights allowed are dim amber ones, mounted low and pointing down, and they go off at 22:00. Amber light is absorbed in the top metre of seawater, so it never reaches the collar.

Seven strategies to hit 70 %

  1. Perforate the deck to at least 35 to 40 % open area in the ring directly over the collar. Shadow edges blur with depth and sun angle, so a 40 % open deck recovers well above 40 % of ambient at 3 to 5 m.
  2. A central oculus or glass floor in each pod. It puts the brightest patch over the pillar head.
  3. Offset the collar to the north and south faces of the pillar. The sun tracks east to west near the equator, so those faces get the least persistent shading.
  4. Keep pod plan area small and spacing wide. Shadow footprint is a direct ecological cost.
  5. Move the shadow. A gap between pods lets the shadow sweep across the collar over the day instead of parking on it.
  6. Pale, high-albedo soffit. A light underside scatters light back down; a dark one is the worst case. Cheap, and a real number for the section.
  7. Instrument it. Two PAR loggers, one shaded and one open reference, near USD 2,000, turns the argument from assertion into a monitored target.

Operable shade, for heatwaves only Design proposition

Shade is contested: it is a legitimate bleaching refuge, and it is a recruitment killer if permanent. Normal condition: at least 70 % ambient light. Heatwave condition, triggered by a DMCR bleaching alert: temporary 30 to 50 % shade cloth over the collars for the duration of the alert only. Two PAR loggers make the switch measurable rather than discretionary.

Night: the dark-sky specification

Two independent bodies of evidence converge on the same fixture. Ayalon et al. (Current Biology 2021) showed corals under low-intensity white and blue LED suffered desynchronised and failed gametogenesis, which is precisely the original blue-LED proposal. Davies et al. (Nature Communications 2023), across 2,135 spawning observations, 156 species and 52 locations, found corals on artificially lit reefs spawned one to three days closer to the full moon than unlit populations, with Porites worst at about three days. Broadcast spawning depends on synchrony, so a pod that shifts local spawning by two days is a reproductive barrier. Separately, hatchling sea-finding is driven by brightness and spectrum, and hatchlings are attracted to short wavelengths. A blue LED on a turtle-nesting island is doubly wrong.

Section 08 · Systems

Section 09 · Time

Operations calendar

The building's year is written by the monsoon, the turtles, the spawning moon and one morning in July, and showing the closures as programme rather than as loss is what makes the project read as professional.

In plain words

The restaurant is not open all year, and that is on purpose. From November to mid-January the monsoon closes it (the platform keeps monitoring the reef). From May to February the lights are fully out at night because turtles are nesting and hatching. Around the full moons of February to May the corals spawn, so lights are out for three nights either side. And on one morning in mid-July, staff walk the exposed reef flat to check that nothing was built above the lowest tide.

Section 10 · Reach

Applicability, reversibility and economics

TURAL is not a coral-reef building, it is a degraded-seabed building, and the same pillar, collar and deck assembly carries a restaurant, a pier, a jetty, a dive shop or a villa with only span, load and services changing.

In plain words

The clever part is not the restaurant; it is the leg. The same leg with its clay collar can hold up a pier, a boat jetty, a dive shop or a holiday villa, anywhere the water is 2 to 6 m deep, calm on one side, warm and clear, and already damaged. When the building's life is over it comes apart in five steps and is carried away; only the collar stays, because by then it is a reef and removing it would be the destructive act. And the collar costs only about 1 to 3 % of the pod, which is the argument for putting one under every shore structure.

Reversibility

The institutional precedent

Rigs-to-Reefs proves regulators already accept that "leave the structure, it has become habitat" can be the better end-of-life outcome, with a financing mechanism around the saving: over 600 platforms reefed in the Gulf of Mexico; reefing costs about half of removal, and the operator donates half the saving to the state reef programme (USD 800,000 removal against 400,000 reefing, 200,000 donated). Sourced

Pre-empt the critique

Rigs-to-Reefs is contested: critics say it lets operators avoid liability and that structures mostly aggregate fish rather than produce them. TURAL's answer is cleaner, and it is a real distinction: the collar was designed as habitat from day one, it is inert fired ceramic rather than steel with a hydrocarbon history, and the building leaves entirely. Only the purpose-built habitat stays.

Section 11 · The field

Benchmarks, awards and entry paths

Juries of architects, scientists and industrialists have already taken this typology seriously, which is the accountability evidence, and the gap they have not yet filled is exactly the one TURAL claims.

In plain words

Other projects have already won big prizes for reef-friendly structures (textured seawalls, printed reef tiles, a half-sunken restaurant in Norway). That proves the idea is taken seriously. But none of them is both a public restaurant and a working coral nursery at the same time. That empty overlap is TURAL's claim to originality, and it is a safe claim because it is a gap, not a boast. For EvE, the most valuable competitions are the ones that accept high-school students: Chula's own high-school competition (which forbids AI-generated work), Bow Seat, GEEP and the Biomimicry Youth Design Challenge.

Reading of the field

No jury has yet awarded a building that is simultaneously a public dining room and an operating coral nursery. Under is a restaurant plus a sea lab. Living Seawalls, Erosion Mitigation Units, rrreefs and ECOncrete are habitat infrastructure without a public programme. That gap is TURAL's actual claim to originality, and it is defensible.

Back cover · The card

The twelve design rules

If the whole book had to fit on one card carried in a pocket to the crit, this is the card. Every rule is a decision the research forced. Break one and you break the argument.

Build only on sand, rubble or dead flat. At least 2 m from live coral. Screw, never hammer. Moorings, never anchors.
The reef collar sits below LAT and spans 2 to 6 m. Fired ceramic, never plain Portland cement on a settlement face.
Two texture scales. 2.5 mm ridges at 7.5 mm pitch, and 4 mm by at least 6 mm open grooves. Vertical or overhung only. Printed sockets for fragments.
At least 70 % of ambient light under the pods. Deck at least 35 to 40 % open over the collars. Pale soffit. Collars on the north and south faces.
Operable shade only, and only on a DMCR bleaching alert.
No underwater light, ever. Exterior amber at 585 nm or longer, full cutoff, mounted at 0.5 m or lower, 1 to 3 lux. Curfew at 22:00 and seasonal lights-out.
Nothing terminates over the reef. Every flow leaves horizontally in the walkway.
No antifouling paint. Copper prohibited. Foul-release on glass and shaft only. Never clean the collar.
Fuel and batteries on land or in the pillar head above splash. No liquid fuel over water.
Every pod carries its sensors. Publish the PAR ratio and the temperature record annually. The table shows this water, now.
Demountable by design. Shell off, deck off, pillar unbolted, pile unscrewed, collar stays.
Say only what you can cite, and label every estimate.

Rules 1 to 3 come from research crews C and F. Rules 4 to 6 come from the light evidence in crew C. Rules 7 to 9 come from crew D's systems work. Rules 10 to 12 are how the project stays honest under questioning.
